# Behavioural self-scoring rubric

Score **before** you look at your notes. Eight lines, 1–4 each. Be harsh — a
generous self-score is a wasted drill, and this is the round where people
believe they did well and do not.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Didn't do it |
| 2 | Did it badly, or only when prompted |
| 3 | Did it competently, unprompted — **the hire bar** |
| 4 | Did it excellently; the interviewer learned something |

## The scorecard

| # | Competency | 3 = hire looks like | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **It was a real situation** | A specific project, a specific quarter, specific people. Not "usually I would" — a hypothetical answer is an admission | |
| 2 | **The stakes were named early** | Inside thirty seconds the listener knows what was at risk and why it was hard. No org-chart preamble | |
| 3 | **You did something** | First person singular where it is true. "We" through an entire answer hides whether you were there | |
| 4 | **There is a number** | Users, latency, money, headcount, weeks. One real figure beats three adjectives | |
| 5 | **A decision, with a cost** | You chose between two defensible options and can say what the losing one would have cost | |
| 6 | **The outcome is honest** | Including when it did not work. An answer where everything went well is either luck or fiction | |
| 7 | **You changed because of it** | Something you do differently now, stated concretely enough that it could be checked | |
| 8 | **It landed in two minutes** | Structured, no restarts, and it stopped. Past three minutes you are narrating | |

**Total /32.**

| Total | Read as |
|---|---|
| < 16 | Not ready — the story is not built yet, not just badly told |
| 16–21 | Told an anecdote. True, and it does not demonstrate anything |
| 22–27 | **Senior.** Specific, owned, measured |
| 28+ | Staff-ish. The interviewer can now describe your judgement to someone else |

## The three lines that most often separate 2 from 3

**#4 — there is a number.** The single most common gap. Most engineers describe
the *shape* of a problem and never its size, so the listener cannot tell a
weekend project from a two-year programme.

**#5 — a decision with a cost.** An answer with no rejected alternative reads as
a task you were assigned, not a judgement you exercised. This is the line that
separates a senior answer from a staff one.

**#7 — you changed.** "I learned a lot" scores 1. "I now write the rollback
plan before the rollout plan" scores 3.

## After scoring — the only three questions that matter

1. **Which competency did I not even attempt?** That is the edit, not the delivery.
2. **Could a listener repeat my decision back to me?** If not, the decision was
   not in the answer.
3. **Which of my six stories should this have been?** Often the answer is a
   better story you did not reach for.

Write the answers in your drill log. A miss that appears in three consecutive
logs is not a gap in preparation; it is a habit, and it needs a rule.

## Why eight lines and not twelve

The [system-design rubric](../07-drills/self-scoring-rubric.md) has twelve
because a design answer has twelve separable parts. A behavioural answer has
eight, and scoring a two-minute story against twelve axes produces noise rather
than signal — you end up grading the telling instead of the substance.
